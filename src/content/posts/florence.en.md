Xu Zhimo rendered Firenze as “翡冷翠,” roughly “cold jade.” Before coming, I had always associated Florence with cold and jade green. We checked into a hotel in the old town at dusk. I opened the window to the neighbors’ shutters and a tree full of green. The name almost fit. Two strange portraits hung in the room, their subjects’ eyes hidden by clouds. I didn’t think much of it. Designer hotel.

The next morning we stepped outside and realized we’d been tricked. A European heat wave had turned the days into high summer. Nothing cold about “cold jade.” Perhaps those clouds in the room were there to bring rain. And even after sunset, the city showed no sign of calling it a night, staying lively until eleven.

## The Dome

To see Florence, you start with the stairs. More than four hundred steps spiraled up Giotto’s Bell Tower, with the staircase growing narrower and the windows smaller as we climbed. Just as I was panting hard enough to question my choices, light came through a wire-mesh window. The cathedral’s red dome was right there, startlingly close. Most of the recognizable buildings seemed connected to the Medici. The cathedral right in front of us was the exception; when construction began, the Medici had yet to come to power.

Down from the bell tower, then into the line for the dome. The way up took us through the middle of a fresco, begun by Vasari and finished by Zuccari: The Last Judgment. From the cathedral floor, it was a blurred ring of color. Up on the walkway, the angels and demons had faces.

![The Last Judgment fresco seen from the walkway inside the dome](https://cdn.theneverless.com/2026/07/florence-last-judgment-fresco.webp)

![Looking up into the painted cupola of Florence Cathedral](https://cdn.theneverless.com/2026/07/florence-cupola-oculus.webp)

The stairs ended and the sky opened, with Florence spread out below us again: red roofs, hills, the river, Palazzo Vecchio, Ponte Vecchio, the Uffizi. I checked the itinerary. Sure enough, we’d be spending more than half of it touring the Medici’s offices and living rooms.

Once we were back down from the dome, there was Palazzo Vecchio right next door, once home to the Medici family. Courtyard frescoes climbed into the vaults. Upstairs, the rooms were dark and every window was bright as a painting. One Gothic window framed the dome perfectly. That picture had been hanging there for five hundred years, unchanged.

## David

From Palazzo Vecchio we walked to the Accademia. David was the biggest name, so we put him ahead of the other galleries. Michelangelo’s unfinished Prisoners lined the corridor, figures still struggling out of the stone. At the far end, under a skylight, stood David. I’d seen him in photographs a hundred times, but standing at his feet still stopped me. He was much taller than I’d imagined, and much younger. The placard said he was meant for the cathedral roof. Instead, he stood outside Palazzo Vecchio for more than three hundred years.

## The Uffizi

From the Accademia we went on to the Uffizi, built by the Medici to house government offices and now a museum. The masterpieces were packed as tightly as the visitors.

![Titian's Venus of Urbino at the Uffizi](https://cdn.theneverless.com/2026/07/florence-uffizi-venus-urbino.webp)

The corridors were worth taking slowly. Their windows were more generous than the galleries. One squeezed Palazzo Vecchio’s tower and the dome into the same frame; another opened onto the entire hillside across the river.

![A marble statue beside a Uffizi window overlooking Florence](https://cdn.theneverless.com/2026/07/florence-uffizi-statue-window.webp)

We left through the Vasari Corridor, once the Medici’s private passage. Its windows faced Ponte Vecchio, where the goldsmiths’ signs baked in the sun. The shops were still selling what they had sold centuries ago. Below us, Florentines came and went across the bridge.

## The Florentines

Out of the museum and down by the river, the Florentines took over. The wind had died, and the bridges and houses dropped whole into the still water. A small boat passed through the reflection, quietly parting the river.

The sun looked ready to set, but there was time yet. We followed the crowd up to Piazzale Michelangelo. Halfway there, the dome and bell tower appeared through a gap in the leaves.

![The cathedral dome and bell tower seen through the leaves](https://cdn.theneverless.com/2026/07/florence-piazzale-skyline.webp)

From the square’s edge, bridges lined up along the Arno toward the edge of the city. Tourists and locals filled the steps and railings, waiting for sunset.

<div class="img-grid">

A scene like this is never short on romance. As the sun neared the ridge, someone proposed. The woman nodded through tears, balloons rose, and a whole square of strangers cheered for them. As the afterglow faded along the horizon, the excitement in the square only kept rising.

![A marriage proposal at sunset in Piazzale Michelangelo](https://cdn.theneverless.com/2026/07/florence-piazzale-proposal-wide.webp)

On the way down, we passed through the rose garden, where the grass was still full of people. Tourists and locals stayed put. The sun was down, but the afterglow still had a long way to go.

![People sitting in the rose garden after sunset](https://cdn.theneverless.com/2026/07/florence-rose-garden.webp)

## Morning and Afterglow

On our last morning I got up early. Florence still belonged to the locals. Light lay low across the flagstones, and a mother cycled past with her child on the way to school. Just another day.

Before leaving, I climbed to the terrace for one last look at the dome. What had stumped builders six hundred years ago was now an everyday sight. The Medici held power, on and off, for three centuries. Nearly three more have passed since their rule ended. What they left is still here, and people still go about their lives in that afterglow. Who once ruled the city doesn’t seem to matter so much anymore.
